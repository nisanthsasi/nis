# KAITHA — Story & Characters

**Logline:** Over one unbroken night on a remote Kerala shore, a woman who has secretly saved for years to escape her marriage kills her abusive husband, uses the sea-guard who adores her to sink the body in the sea — then burns it all down and vanishes with the money, leaving him only a voice note.

**Spine:** *The savings were always the exit; the love was the accident.* She loves the man she leaves, and she leaves him anyway. Love real, betrayal real — fused.

---

## THE STORY

A hot, windless night on a rock-strewn stretch of the southern-Kerala coast. At the very end of the open shore, where the sand gives way to boulders and thorny **kaitha** (screwpine), stands a single low hut. Beyond it the ground climbs to a black cliff over the sea.

A man, **KRISH**, walks up out of the surf. The screwpine snags his leg and draws a thin line of blood — the shore taking its small tax. He washes the salt off at the water vessels by the hut as a woman's voice calls from the dark behind it: *Is that you?* — *It's me.* He goes inside. And we leave him there, drifting instead toward the backyard, where **THANKAM** is finishing her bath in the moonlight, not knowing she's watched. From this moment the camera belongs to her and never leaves.

Inside, she lights the single kerosene lamp and the bare hut warms into something that looks, for an hour, like a home. Krish is comfortable here; he's been coming a long time. They are lovers. They eat, they talk, they make love. Afterward he dreams aloud the dream he always dreams — a patch of land down south, a well already dug, the two of them taking the boat one morning and simply never coming back. *Soon,* he says. She says it back, and she means it, which is the unbearable part.

Then the ritual: he gives her the week's roll of cash — his money, quietly, the kind a sea-guard earns by *not seeing* the night boats — and she tucks it into a clay rice pot on the high shelf, down among a dozen older rolls hidden in the grain. He calls it *our field, our savings.* He has never once counted it. He doesn't understand that the shelf is hers, the pot is hers, and only her hand goes into the rice. Before he leaves for his midnight round he swaps her a secretly-charged phone for her dead one — no power reaches this hut — and tells her: *anyone comes, you call me. Only me.* He goes out into the dark.

She is alone with the curry on the fire and the money on the shelf. Then footsteps — wrong ones, heavy, from the road side, not the sea. **RAMANAN** fills the doorway. He doesn't knock; you don't knock at your own house. The faded marriage chain at her throat tells us what the last hour hid: this is her husband.

He's drunk, ruined — a man who once owned a blue fishing boat and a future, both long drowned. He eats the food she cooked. For one breath he is almost gentle, remembering the boat, the days the whole shore watched them. Then the softness sours, the way it always does. He wants her to *be there,* to fear him, to prove he still exists in this house — and when she gives him nothing, only the stone stillness of a woman who decided years ago not to flinch, he hits her. She does not react. Not a sound. He waits for the crying that is his only proof of himself; it doesn't come. Ashamed, he goes out to wash.

He comes back worse. He grabs her; this time, for the first time, she pushes back. The fight is fast and ugly and close, and when he flings her off she goes into the high shelf — and the rice pot falls and shatters. The money spills across the floor, a dozen soft rolls rocking to a stop at his feet. He stares. It reaches him slowly through the drink: more cash than he's held in years, hidden in his own house, kept from him. He doesn't think *where from.* He thinks *mine,* and drops to his knees and starts stuffing her years, her exit, into his waistband. And the cold door that has been shut in her for a lifetime swings fully open. She takes a billet of firewood from the hearth and kills him. We watch it as two giant shadows on the lamplit wall.

Then the horror — real and enormous. She killed the blue-boat man. The one torn sound she makes all night. But underneath the panic, the other thing surfaces, the thing that was always there: the architect, returning to her table. She recovers every note from the blood and the rice. The body is too heavy to move alone; she was never going to do this alone. She looks at the open door, the sea, the moon — and the plan assembles itself out of the pieces she already owns: a man who loves her, who guards this coast, who has a boat. She takes the secret phone — the one he keeps alive for her — and calls him. She holds her voice level, which is the hardest thing she does all night. *Come back. Now.*

He runs the whole way. He steps into the doorway and stops dead at the body, the rice, the spilled money. And he sees that money — *his* money, the rolls he carried here week by week — and for half a second the whole truth stands in front of him: the husband didn't die for the hands at her throat; he died when he got his hands on the cash. Krish looks at it, looks at her — and chooses not to see it, because the man who finishes that thought can't do what comes next, and he has already decided to do it. He loves her past the truth, the way he's about to love her past the law. He wraps the body in a reed mat, puts on his uniform — the guard, the protector of the coast — and motors it out past the third bar, where nothing comes back. He promises her again: *we're alright, you and me, soon.* She gives him the word back. The camera stays on the shore. It does not go with him.

And while he's out on the black water, she works. She packs a small bundle — nothing of the life, nothing of either man — and the money, all of it. She pours kerosene through the hut: the bed, the straight shelves, the dead television. She lifts the lamp she lit for his wounded leg, the warm heart of the whole warm lie, and she climbs. The kaitha hooks her leg as it hooked his in the first minute; she frees it with the same two fingers. The shore takes its tax from her too.

From the cliff she can see everything: the moon road, the far useless street lights, the small dark hut, and the boat-light turning now, coming home to her. She takes out the phone, records a few words, and sends them. Then she throws the lamp. It turns once in the dark and breaks open on the soaked threshold, and the hut takes the fire completely — the only truly bright thing in the whole film, warm gold flooding the cold night. The boat-light stops, then races for shore. Krish stumbles out into the surf and stares up at the fire, far too late, and lifts the phone to his ear. We don't hear it; we already have. *Krishéttaa… not all of it was a lie.*

And then the camera turns back to her — and she is already going, the bundle on her shoulder, away from the cliff, away from the fire, into the dark. She doesn't look back at the hut. She doesn't look back at the man. She walks out of the frame, and we do not follow. We are left exactly where he is left: with the fire, the sea, and the small figure who thought he was building a bridge. After a long moment — the first and only cut in the film — black.

## THE CHARACTERS

### THANKAM — *the architect* (protagonist)
*Her name means "gold." She is the only one who knows where the gold is.*

**Who she is:** A woman in her thirties on a lonely coast, married young to Ramanan when he was strong and had a boat and a future. The sea took the boat; drink took the man; and the years of being beaten, used, and provided-for-by-no-one taught her the only lesson she ever needed: **no one is coming to free her.** So she built her own way out — quietly, patiently, the way she does everything — taking a sea-guard lover who pays, and hoarding every rupee in a rice pot against the morning she disappears. She did not plan to love him. She does. The plan did not change.

- **Want (external):** vanish, with the money, before dawn — be free.
- **Need (internal):** to stop pricing herself by what men give and take; to *own herself.*
- **Wound:** every bond she's known has been ownership; love has always been a cage.
- **Flaw:** she believes safety requires absolute solitude — that to be free she must betray even the love that is real.
- **Contradiction:** she truly loves Krish *and* always meant to leave him; she nurtures — curry, care, the kept savings — the very men she's escaping.
- **The secret she carries the whole night:** tonight is the night. Every warm thing she does is genuine *and* is the last time.
- **Arc:** a woman who survives by serving men's appetites → a woman who *acts,* kills, chooses herself, and walks out of the frame — out of the gaze, out of ownership.

**Crucial:** she is neither victim nor villain. She is both at once. Play the warmth real; let the audience *re-read* her at the end rather than catch her early. Her stillness under violence is a discipline, not numbness. Her one fully honest touch of the night is for the money, not the man — and even that is grief.

---

### KRISH — *the lover / sea-guard*
*A decent man's heart inside a corrupt man's job — the most dangerous combination.*

**Who he is:** A coast sea-guard in his late thirties who long ago began taking money to look away from the night boats. Lonely, and good in his own telling, he fell for Thankam and turned the corruption into a courtship: the weekly rolls, *for our field;* the secretly-charged phone he keeps alive for her; the dream of land down south. He believes, with his whole chest, that the savings are *theirs* and that this is a love story with a future.

- **Want:** take her away; build a life *together.*
- **Need:** to be *chosen,* not used.
- **Flaw:** devotion blinds him; he reads her care as love *for him.*
- **Contradiction:** a guardian of the coast who becomes a body-disposer for love; an officer of the law who is himself a criminal.
- **His defining beat:** seeing his own bribe money in the blood and understanding everything — and *choosing not to,* on purpose, because the truth would stop him saving her.
- **Arc:** arrives a happy lover → ends abandoned inside the frame, an accomplice on a dark beach holding a voice note, every *soon* he ever said going out with the tide. He is the price she pays for her freedom.

**Crucial:** never play him stupid. Play him *in love past the truth.* He damns himself gladly, eagerly — that's the tragedy.

---

### RAMANAN — *the husband*
*A drowned man who still claims the shore.*

**Who he is:** A fisherman in his fifties who reads older, a once-powerful body gone soft and mean with arrack. He owned a blue boat and a place on the shore and a wife who sat in the bow while the village watched — and the sea took the boat and the drink took the rest, and now he has nothing left to own but Thankam and the hut, the last things he can call his.

- **Want:** dominion — food, sex, the house, control.
- **Need (unmet):** to *matter;* to be feared, because fear is the last proof he exists.
- **Flaw:** entitlement and violence.
- **Contradiction:** he provides nothing and claims everything — yet carries one true flicker of the man he was.
- **His function:** the detonator. He never knows about the lover; he learns of the money only when it spills, and dies moments later, grabbing it.
- **Arc:** predator → corpse — the night's first power inversion.

**Crucial:** never a cartoon brute. For one breath — the blue boat, *where'd it go, Thankam* — the audience must almost mourn him. His violence comes from shame, not relish; he hits her because she has stopped being afraid, and her fearlessness is the one thing he can't survive.

---

## THE THREE OF THEM (relationships & the secret map)
- **Thankam ↔ Krish:** real love, built on a transaction, doomed by her plan. He thinks they're escaping *together;* she's using him to escape *him.*
- **Thankam ↔ Ramanan:** a dead marriage of ownership; the cage she's tunneling out of. He is what she's escaping; the lover is the softer version of the same cage.
- **The money** is the silent fourth character: Krish's dirty bribe cash, kept by Thankam as her exit fund, found by Ramanan, killed over, and carried out the door by the only person who ever understood what it was *for.*
